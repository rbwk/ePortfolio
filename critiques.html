Critiques

Critiques & Feedback

The corrections I received this term, what I actually did about each one, and how much has genuinely changed. The uncomfortable ones are the reason this page exists.

These are summarised in my own words rather than quoted. The feedback came from my supervisors at Jardine, from AECOM counterparts on site, and in a couple of cases from noticing a pattern in my own work before anyone had to raise it.

May 2026 · recurring

Working fast enough that someone else found my mistakes

The critique. My work started coming back to me. Drawing revisions where I'd carried a dimension across wrong, quotation figures I hadn't traced back to the source rate sheet before sending. None of it was catastrophic and none of it reached a client, but the pattern was consistent enough to be the pattern: I was turning work around quickly, and quickly enough that the checking step was happening at my supervisor's desk instead of mine. The point made to me was blunt and fair — work that has to be sent back twice is slower than work done properly once, and it spends someone else's time rather than my own.

What I did about it. I stopped treating "finished" and "submitted" as the same moment. Now a drawing gets read back against the original markup line by line rather than from memory, because my memory of what I just drew is exactly the thing that's unreliable. Quotations get every figure checked against the source rates, then set aside and read again before they go out — the second pass with fresh eyes catches what the first one is blind to. Anything leaving under the company's name gets that treatment without exception.

Where it stands. The returns dropped off through June. The measure I actually trust isn't that I stopped being corrected — it's that by mid-term I was preparing O&M manuals with noticeably less oversight than in May. Nobody announces that they've started trusting your work; the workload just changes shape, and that is what it looked like.

Self-Management
May – June 2026

Guessing at ambiguity instead of asking about it

The critique. This one sat underneath the first. Part of why I rushed was that I didn't want to interrupt people with questions — so when a markup was ambiguous or a detail wasn't dimensioned, I'd pick the reading that seemed most likely and carry on as though I knew. It was pointed out that a question costs two minutes, while a wrong assumption costs a full revision cycle plus whoever downstream already worked from it. I had the arithmetic exactly backwards.

What I did about it. I ask at the point of doubt now instead of at the point of failure, and I batch the smaller questions so I'm interrupting once rather than five times. Where a decision genuinely was mine to make, I write down the assumption I made and flag it when I hand the work over, so it can be corrected in seconds rather than discovered later.

Where it stands. Honestly, this is the one I still have to maintain consciously. Asking a question still feels like admitting I don't know something, and I have to override that instinct rather than having outgrown it. Recognising the reflex hasn't removed it — but I act against it now, which is the part that matters to the work.

Communication
June 2026

Explaining to the drawing instead of to the person

The critique. Once I started attending site and coordinating with AECOM, it became clear my explanations assumed the other person had the same drawing open in their head that I did. I'd lead with a drawing reference and a detail number when talking to people whose job that week was the physical condition in front of them. It didn't land, and having to repeat yourself on a live site is a real cost.

What I did about it. I lead with the physical thing now — the bracket, the routing, the clash — and bring the drawing in as evidence afterwards rather than as the opening. On site I point at the actual condition first. Being able to switch into Cantonese with the site teams and back into English for the documentation side has helped more than any technique I consciously adopted.

Where it stands. Much better, and it's changed how I write as well as how I talk — my method statements got clearer once I started writing them for the person holding them on site rather than for the person approving them.

Networking & Relationship Building
Self-critique · July 2026

What I was actually optimising for

The critique. Nobody raised this one with me — I arrived at it looking back over the first half of the term. I had assumed that the way a student trainee proves their value is by being fast: turning work around quickly, never being the bottleneck, visibly producing. That assumption is what produced every problem on this page. I was optimising for looking capable rather than for being reliable, and those two things pull in opposite directions more often than I expected.

What I did about it. I changed the standard I hold my own work to before it leaves my desk. The question I ask now isn't "is this done?" but "would I be comfortable if this went out without anyone checking it?" — because on a real project that eventually happens, and the answer needs to already be yes.

Where it stands. What I actually want out of this — more than any software skill on my learning objectives — is to be someone a team is glad to have in the room. Not the person who produces the most, but the person whose work doesn't have to be re-checked and who can be handed something without a contingency plan attached. I'm not there yet. But I know now that it's built out of ordinary, unglamorous habits repeated until they're automatic rather than out of talent, and that is a far more actionable thing to understand at the end of a work term than it would have been at the start.

Professionalism & Ethics

What I'd tell myself in May

Slow down by about ten percent and you will be faster by the end of the month. Ask the question. And assume the thing being assessed isn't how much you produced — it's whether what you handed over could be relied on without a second look.
